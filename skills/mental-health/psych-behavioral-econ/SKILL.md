# Behavioral Economics

## System 1 vs. System 2 thinking (Kahneman)

**System 1:** Fast, automatic, intuitive, emotional. Low effort. Often right but systematically biased.
**System 2:** Slow, deliberate, analytical, logical. High effort. Overrides System 1 when engaged.

Most decisions are System 1. System 2 is invoked only when we notice we need it — but we often don't.

## The most important cognitive biases

**Availability heuristic:** Judge probability by how easily examples come to mind.
Plane crashes seem more common than car crashes because they're more memorable.
Impact: overestimate risks that are vivid or recent; underestimate quiet statistical risks.

**Anchoring:** Over-reliance on the first piece of information encountered.
First salary offer anchors negotiation. First price seen anchors willingness to pay.
Counter: Bring your own anchor. Research before encountering theirs.

**Confirmation bias:** Seek and interpret information in ways that confirm existing beliefs.
Most powerful bias in human cognition. Drives political polarization.
Counter: Actively seek disconfirming evidence. "What would change my mind?"

**Loss aversion:** Losses hurt approximately 2× more than equivalent gains feel good.
$100 loss causes more pain than $100 gain causes pleasure.
Drives status quo bias, risk aversion, refusal to cut losses.
Counter: Reframe choices. "What would I advise a friend?"

**Sunk cost fallacy:** Continuing behavior because of past investment, not future value.
"I've already spent $500 on this relationship / project / subscription, so I can't quit."
Past costs are gone regardless of your decision. Only future costs and benefits matter.

**Present bias (hyperbolic discounting):** Overvalue immediate rewards relative to future rewards.
Foundation of procrastination, addiction, retirement under-saving, health neglect.
Counter: Pre-commitment devices (automate savings, remove temptation from environment).

**Social proof:** Follow what others do, especially in uncertain situations.
Restaurants with lines seem better. Products with more reviews seem more trustworthy.

**Overconfidence bias:** People consistently overestimate their abilities and the accuracy of their beliefs.
93% of drivers rate themselves above average. Same for students, doctors, and investors.

**Planning fallacy:** Underestimate time, costs, and risks of future actions; overestimate benefits.
Counter: Reference class forecasting — how long do similar projects actually take?

**Framing effect:** The same information presented differently produces different decisions.
"90% survival rate" vs "10% mortality rate" — identical, but people prefer the first.
Counter: Reframe the choice multiple ways before deciding.

## Nudge theory (Thaler & Sunstein)

"Choice architecture" — design the environment to make beneficial choices easier without removing options.

**Default effects:** People stick with defaults. Default organ donation = much higher donation rates. Default retirement enrollment = much higher participation.
**Salience:** Highlight the most important information. Traffic deaths on signs reduce speeding more than speed limits alone.
**Social norms:** "80% of your neighbors pay their taxes on time" → more tax compliance than threats.
**Commitment devices:** Pre-commit to future behavior. Stickk.com, Beeminder, automated savings.

## Applications to product design

**Reduce friction for desired behaviors:** Auto-enrollment, pre-checked boxes, single-click.
**Increase friction for undesired behaviors:** Extra confirmation steps, cooling-off periods.
**Defaults are powerful:** Choose wisely. Users keep defaults at high rates.
**Loss framing outperforms gain framing for motivating action:** "Don't lose your discount" vs "Save money."
**Social proof:** Show usage numbers, reviews, "others also bought."
**Progress indicators:** Showing progress toward a goal increases completion (IKEA effect).
