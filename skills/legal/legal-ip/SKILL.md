# Intellectual Property Law

## The four types of IP protection

| Type             | Protects                       | Duration                 | Registration                          |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------ | ------------------------------------- |
| **Patent**       | Inventions, processes, designs | 20 years (utility)       | Required                              |
| **Trademark**    | Brand names, logos, slogans    | Indefinitely (if used)   | Not required but strongly recommended |
| **Copyright**    | Creative works                 | Life + 70 years          | Not required but recommended          |
| **Trade Secret** | Confidential business info     | Indefinitely (if secret) | Not available                         |

## Copyright

### What copyright protects automatically

Copyright attaches the moment an original work is fixed in tangible form.
No registration required. No © symbol required (but use it).

**Protected works:**

- Literary works (including software code)
- Music and lyrics
- Visual art, photography, illustrations
- Films, videos
- Architecture (original elements)
- Websites, written content

**Not protected by copyright:**

- Ideas, concepts, facts
- Names, titles, slogans (trademark protects these)
- Government works (US federal government works are public domain)
- Works with expired copyright

### Copyright registration

Why register even though not required:

- Enables lawsuit for infringement
- Statutory damages ($750–$150,000 per work if registered before infringement)
- Attorney fees recoverable
- Public record of ownership

File at copyright.gov. Cost: $35–$65 per work.

### Fair Use (17 U.S.C. § 107)

Use of copyrighted material may be fair use based on four factors:

1. **Purpose:** Transformative? Commercial or educational?
2. **Nature:** Factual or creative? (Facts = more likely fair use)
3. **Amount:** How much used relative to the whole?
4. **Market effect:** Does it substitute for the original market?

Fair use is a defense, not a right. It is determined case by case.
When in doubt, license or use public domain alternatives.

### Copyright in the workplace

Work made for hire: employer owns copyright for:

- Works created by employees within scope of employment
- Commissioned works if written agreement says "work for hire"
  (only for specific categories: contributions to collective works, etc.)

**Independent contractors do NOT automatically transfer copyright.**
Must have written assignment in the contract.

### DMCA (Digital Millennium Copyright Act)

- Takedown notices: if your content is used without permission, send DMCA notice to the platform
- Safe harbor: platforms not liable for user infringement IF they respond promptly to takedowns
- Anti-circumvention: illegal to bypass technological protection measures (DRM)

## Trademarks

### What trademarks protect

A trademark identifies the source of goods or services.
Can be: words, names, logos, symbols, colors, sounds, smells (rare).

**Strength hierarchy (strongest to weakest):**

1. Fanciful: made-up words (KODAK, XEROX) — strongest protection
2. Arbitrary: real words with unrelated meaning (APPLE for computers)
3. Suggestive: hints at the product (NETFLIX, COPPERTONE)
4. Descriptive: describes the product — weak, needs secondary meaning
5. Generic: the product category name — never protectable

### Common law vs. registered trademark

**Common law:** Use ™ symbol. Rights based on actual use in commerce.
Geographic scope limited to where you actually operate.

**Registered trademark:** Use ® symbol after USPTO registration.
Nationwide priority as of filing date.
Constructive notice to all users.
Basis for customs enforcement against counterfeits.
Enables federal lawsuit.

### USPTO registration process

1. Search (TESS database at uspto.gov) for conflicting marks
2. File application: $250–$350 per class online
3. USPTO examination: 3-6 months
4. Publication for opposition: 30 days for others to object
5. Registration: if no opposition, ~8-12 months total

**Classes:** Trademarks are registered per class of goods/services.
Register in every class where you use the mark. (45 classes total)

### Trademark maintenance

- Use it or lose it. Must maintain actual use in commerce.
- File Sections 8 & 9 between years 5-6 (showing continued use)
- Renew every 10 years
- Police your mark. Failing to stop infringement weakens your rights.

## Patents

### Types of patents

**Utility patent:** Protects functional inventions — processes, machines, compositions, methods.
20-year term from filing date. Most common type.

**Design patent:** Protects ornamental appearance of a functional item.
15-year term from grant. Less expensive than utility.

**Plant patent:** Protects asexually reproduced plant varieties.

### What is patentable?

Must be:

- **Novel:** Not publicly known or used before filing
- **Non-obvious:** Not obvious to someone skilled in the field
- **Useful:** Has practical utility
- **Patentable subject matter:** Process, machine, manufacture, or composition

**Cannot patent:**

- Laws of nature, natural phenomena
- Abstract ideas (but software implementing them may be patentable)
- Mathematical formulas alone

### The one-year rule (US)

If you publicly disclose, use, sell, or offer to sell your invention,
you have ONE YEAR to file a patent application in the US.
International: must file BEFORE any public disclosure (no grace period in most countries).

**File a provisional application first** ($320 for small entity).
Establishes your filing date. Gives 12 months to file the full application.
Not examined, not published. Placeholder only.

### Patent process (utility)

1. Provisional application → establishes priority date
2. Non-provisional application ($800–$1,600 + attorney fees)
3. Patent examination: 2-3 years average
4. Office actions: USPTO rejections you must respond to
5. Grant (if approved) + maintenance fees every 3.5, 7.5, 11.5 years

Cost: DIY provisional ~$300-500 / Full application with attorney $10,000–$30,000

## Trade Secrets

### What qualifies as a trade secret

Information that:

1. Has independent economic value from being secret
2. Is subject to reasonable measures to keep it secret

Examples: formulas (Coca-Cola), algorithms, customer lists, business methods,
manufacturing processes, pricing strategies, source code.

### Protecting trade secrets

Required steps to maintain trade secret status:

- **NDAs** with all employees, contractors, visitors who see confidential info
- **Access controls:** Only share with those who need to know
- **Physical security:** Lock up confidential documents
- **Digital security:** Password protection, encryption, access logs
- **Exit procedures:** Remind departing employees of obligations, collect devices

**Failure to protect = loss of trade secret status.**
Courts have denied protection when companies were sloppy about confidentiality.

### Trade secret misappropriation

Illegal acquisition through improper means:

- Theft, bribery, breach of NDA
- Hacking or unauthorized computer access
- Inducing someone to breach their confidentiality obligation

Federal law: Defend Trade Secrets Act (DTSA) — provides federal cause of action.
Remedies: injunction, damages, attorney fees, in egregious cases: treble damages.

## Open Source Licenses — quick guide

| License    | Can use commercially | Must release source     | Must use same license     |
| ---------- | -------------------- | ----------------------- | ------------------------- |
| MIT        | ✅                   | ❌                      | ❌                        |
| Apache 2.0 | ✅                   | ❌                      | ❌                        |
| BSD        | ✅                   | ❌                      | ❌                        |
| GPL v2/v3  | ✅                   | ✅ (if distributed)     | ✅ (copyleft)             |
| AGPL       | ✅                   | ✅ (including SaaS)     | ✅ (copyleft)             |
| LGPL       | ✅                   | ✅ (LGPL portions only) | Partial                   |
| CC-BY      | ✅                   | ❌                      | ❌ (attribution required) |
| CC-BY-NC   | ❌ commercial        | ❌                      | ❌                        |

**For ORIRO:** Use MIT, Apache 2.0, CC-BY only. Never AGPL in commercial product.

## IP in Employment and Contracts

**Employee IP assignment:**
Most employment agreements include broad IP assignment.
Employees must assign work-related IP to employer.

**Carveout provision** (employees should negotiate):
"Excluding inventions I can prove were developed on my own time,
without using company equipment, and outside the scope of company business."
California Labor Code § 2870 provides this protection by law.

**Contractor IP:**
Contractors retain copyright unless a written agreement assigns it.
Always include in contracts:
"Contractor assigns all right, title, and interest in work product
to Client upon payment of all fees."
