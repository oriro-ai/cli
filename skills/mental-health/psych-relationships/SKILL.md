# Relationship Psychology

## Attachment theory (Bowlby, Ainsworth)

Early attachment patterns shape adult relationships.

**Secure attachment:** Comfortable with intimacy and independence. Trusts partner. Can communicate needs.
**Anxious attachment:** Craves closeness, fears abandonment. Hyperactivates attachment system (clinginess, jealousy, reassurance-seeking).
**Avoidant attachment:** Values independence, uncomfortable with closeness. Deactivates attachment system (emotional distance, self-reliance to extreme).
**Disorganized attachment:** Combination of anxious and avoidant. Often trauma-based. Fears both intimacy and abandonment.

**These are patterns, not diagnoses.** Can shift with secure relationships and therapy.

## The Gottman Four Horsemen (predictors of relationship failure)

John Gottman's research: these four communication patterns predict relationship dissolution with 90%+ accuracy.

**1. Criticism:** Attacking character rather than behavior.
"You never listen. You're so selfish." (vs. complaint about specific behavior)
Antidote: Gentle startup. "I feel [X] when [Y]. I need [Z]."

**2. Contempt:** Superiority, mockery, eye-rolling, sarcasm, name-calling.
Most toxic predictor of relationship failure. Communicates "I'm better than you."
Antidote: Build culture of appreciation. Express gratitude regularly.

**3. Defensiveness:** Self-protection through counterattack or victim stance.
"That's not my fault, you started it!" Prevents hearing the partner's concern.
Antidote: Take responsibility for even a small part. "You're right that I..."

**4. Stonewalling:** Withdrawing from interaction — shutting down, leaving, silence.
Often occurs after flooding (physiological overwhelm).
Antidote: Physiological self-soothing. Take a 20-minute break. Return to discussion.

**The antidote to failure:** 5:1 ratio — five positive interactions for every negative interaction. Build emotional bank account.

## Effective communication patterns

**Non-violent communication (NVC — Rosenberg):**
Four components:

1. **Observation:** What you actually saw/heard (no evaluation). "When I see dishes left in the sink..."
2. **Feeling:** How you feel about it. "...I feel frustrated..."
3. **Need:** What need is underlying the feeling. "...because I value shared responsibility..."
4. **Request:** Concrete, doable, present-tense request. "...Would you be willing to wash your dishes tonight?"

**Active listening:**
Reflect back what you heard before responding. "What I hear you saying is... Is that right?"
Ask clarifying questions before defending.
Validate the emotion even if you disagree with the conclusion: "I can understand why you'd feel that way."

**Repair attempts:** Any attempt to de-escalate during conflict. Can be silly ("I love you even when I'm mad"). Research shows successful repairs predict relationship stability more than conflict itself.

## Boundaries

A boundary is a limit you set to protect your values, needs, and wellbeing.
Not: "You can't do that." (controlling the other person)
Yes: "If you do X, I will do Y." (what you will do in response)

**Setting boundaries:**

1. Identify what you need / what is non-negotiable.
2. Communicate clearly and directly. Avoid hinting.
3. State the consequence you will implement (not a threat — an honest statement of your planned action).
4. Follow through. Boundaries without consequences are not boundaries.

**Why boundaries are hard:** Fear of conflict, fear of losing the relationship, guilt, cultural conditioning, unclear about own needs.

## Relationship health indicators

**Healthy relationship signs:** Mutual respect, effective conflict resolution, safety to be yourself, maintained individual identity, shared and separate interests, ability to discuss problems, physical and emotional safety.
**Warning signs:** Walking on eggshells, isolation from friends/family, feeling monitored or controlled, fear of partner's reaction to normal events, gaslighting (being told your perceptions aren't real), threats or intimidation, financial control.

## Conflict resolution

**Three approaches (Thomas-Kilmann):**
Compete: Win at the other's expense. Appropriate for crises; damages relationships long-term.
Accommodate: Give in. Appropriate occasionally; creates resentment if habitual.
Collaborate: Find solution meeting both parties' needs. Takes longer; sustains relationships.
Compromise: Both get some but not all. Acceptable middle ground.
Avoid: Neither party gets needs met. Sometimes appropriate for low-stakes issues; never for high-stakes.

**Most relationship conflicts are perpetual (69% per Gottman).** The goal is not resolution but dialogue and understanding, managing the conflict without it destroying the relationship.
