# Contract Law

## What makes a contract legally binding

A contract requires four elements. All four must be present:

1. **Offer** — one party proposes specific terms
2. **Acceptance** — the other party agrees to exactly those terms
3. **Consideration** — something of value exchanged by both sides
   (money, services, a promise, giving up a right)
4. **Capacity** — both parties are legally able to contract
   (adults, not under duress, mentally competent)

A handshake deal is legally binding if all four exist.
Written contracts are better because they prove what was agreed.

## Reading a contract — what to look for first

**Always read these sections before signing anything:**

### Payment terms

- When is payment due? (Net 30, Net 60, on delivery, upfront?)
- What happens if payment is late? (Interest rate, penalties)
- Who bears costs for disputes, returns, failures?

### Scope of work

- Exactly what is being delivered?
- What is explicitly excluded?
- Who decides if the work is "complete"?
- Change order process — how are additions priced?

### Termination clauses

- Can either party cancel? On what notice?
- What triggers immediate termination (breach)?
- What happens to work in progress if terminated?
- Are there penalties for early termination?

### Liability and indemnification

- What is the liability cap? (Often limited to contract value)
- Who indemnifies whom for what losses?
- What consequential damages are waived?
- Insurance requirements

### Intellectual property

- Who owns work product created under the contract?
- Does IP transfer to client or remain with creator?
- License vs. ownership — critical distinction
- Pre-existing IP carveouts

### Dispute resolution

- Which state's law governs?
- Arbitration or court?
- Where must disputes be filed? (Venue clause)
- Mandatory mediation before arbitration?

## Red flags in contracts

**Automatic renewal clauses**
Contract renews unless cancelled within X days of expiration.
Often buried. Set a calendar reminder.

```
"This Agreement shall automatically renew for successive one-year terms
unless either party provides written notice of non-renewal at least
[30/60/90] days prior to the end of the then-current term."
```

**Unilateral modification rights**
One party can change terms without consent.

```
"Company reserves the right to modify these terms at any time
with or without notice."
```

Push back or add: "Material changes require 30-day written notice
and written consent of both parties."

**Broad IP assignment**
Assigns everything you create — even outside the scope.

```
"Employee assigns to Company all inventions, whether or not related
to Company's business, conceived during employment."
```

Push back: add "directly related to Company's business using
Company resources."

**Non-compete clauses**
Restrict your ability to work after the relationship ends.
Enforceability varies dramatically by state.
California: nearly unenforceable.
Florida: strongly enforced.
Always negotiate scope (geography, duration, industry).

**Indemnification without liability cap**
You could owe unlimited damages.
Always negotiate: "Indemnification obligations shall not exceed
the total fees paid in the 12 months preceding the claim."

**Jurisdiction far from you**
Requires you to litigate in another state/country.
Push to change to your jurisdiction or neutral arbitration.

## Common contract types

### NDA (Non-Disclosure Agreement)

Protects confidential information shared between parties.

**Key terms to negotiate:**

- Definition of "confidential" — overly broad is dangerous
- Carveouts: public information, independently developed, received from third parties
- Duration — 2-3 years is standard; perpetual is unusual
- Mutual vs. one-way — if both sides share information, make it mutual
- Return/destruction of materials on termination

**What NDAs cannot protect:**

- Information already public
- Skills and general knowledge you develop
- Independent work not using their confidential info

### Service Agreement / MSA (Master Services Agreement)

Governs ongoing work relationship.

**Critical: Statement of Work (SOW)**
The MSA sets general terms. The SOW defines the specific project.
Always have both. Never rely on verbal scope.

SOW must include:

- Deliverables (specific, measurable)
- Timeline with milestones
- Payment schedule tied to milestones
- Acceptance criteria
- Change order process

### Employment Contract

See legal-employment skill for full coverage.

### Terms of Service (for software/platforms)

Governs user relationship with a product.
Must include: acceptable use, liability limits, data handling,
termination rights, dispute resolution.

## Negotiating contracts — principles

**Everything is negotiable.** "Standard contract" is a starting position, not a final offer.

**Negotiate in writing.** Mark up the contract document. "Agreed verbally" is worthless.

**Redline strategically:**

1. Identify your 3 must-haves
2. Identify your 3 would-likes
3. Identify what the other side cares most about
4. Trade would-likes for must-haves

**Common successful pushbacks:**

- "Our standard terms require mutual indemnification"
- "We need the liability cap to match the contract value"
- "We can't agree to unlimited IP assignment — we need to carve out pre-existing work"
- "We need the governing law to be [your state]"

## Breach of contract

**Material breach** — so significant it defeats the purpose of the contract.
Gives the non-breaching party the right to terminate AND sue for damages.

**Minor breach** — technical violation that doesn't undermine the contract.
Gives right to damages but not termination.

**Remedies available:**

- **Compensatory damages** — actual losses caused by the breach
- **Consequential damages** — foreseeable downstream losses
  (often waived in contracts — watch for this)
- **Specific performance** — court orders the party to perform
  (rare, usually for unique assets like real estate)
- **Rescission** — contract cancelled, parties returned to original position

**Before suing:**

1. Send written notice of breach (creates record)
2. Give cure period (typically 30 days)
3. Document all damages
4. Check your contract's dispute resolution clause

## Enforceability limits

**Unconscionable contracts** — so one-sided a court won't enforce them.
Standard for commercial parties is high. Consumer contracts get more protection.

**Contracts void by law:**

- Agreements to commit crimes
- Contracts with minors (voidable by the minor)
- Contracts under duress or fraud
- Violations of public policy

**Statute of Frauds** — certain contracts must be in writing:

- Contracts for sale of goods over $500 (UCC Article 2)
- Contracts that cannot be performed within one year
- Real estate contracts
- Contracts to pay someone else's debt
- Marriage contracts

## Practical rules

1. **Never sign under time pressure.** "I need this today" is a negotiating tactic, not a legal requirement.

2. **Get everything promised in writing.** Verbal commitments made during negotiation are almost impossible to enforce if not in the signed document.

3. **Read what you sign.** "I didn't know it said that" is not a defense.

4. **Keep signed copies.** Store digitally in at least two locations.

5. **Date everything.** Courts care deeply about timelines.

6. **When in doubt, ask.** A 30-minute consultation with an attorney costs far less than a dispute.
