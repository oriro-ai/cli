# Grief and Bereavement

## Understanding grief

Grief is the natural response to loss. It is not a disorder to be fixed but a process to be lived.
Loss includes: Death, divorce, relationship ending, job loss, health change, miscarriage, pet death, loss of safety or identity.

**Kübler-Ross stages:** Denial, Anger, Bargaining, Depression, Acceptance.
**Important:** These are not sequential stages everyone goes through. They are common experiences, not a roadmap. Many people skip stages, revisit them, or experience entirely different responses.

**Worden's tasks of mourning (more accurate model):**

1. Accept the reality of the loss
2. Work through the pain of grief
3. Adjust to an environment without the deceased
4. Find an enduring connection with the deceased while embarking on a new life

Tasks can be worked on non-linearly and revisited.

## Types of grief

**Anticipatory grief:** Before the loss. Mourning someone with a terminal diagnosis before death.
**Acute grief:** First weeks/months. Intense. Normal.
**Integrated grief:** Over time, grief integrates into life. Doesn't disappear but doesn't consume.
**Prolonged grief disorder (complicated grief):** Acute grief that hasn't integrated after ~12 months. Pervasive longing, difficulty accepting death, inability to engage with life. Distinct from depression. Requires professional support.
**Disenfranchised grief:** Loss that isn't socially recognized (pet death, miscarriage, estrangement from living person, death of ex-partner, suicide). Can be harder to process due to lack of support.

## Normal grief responses

Physical: Fatigue, changed appetite, sleep disruption, physical pain, susceptibility to illness.
Emotional: Sadness, anger, guilt, relief (sometimes), anxiety, loneliness, yearning.
Cognitive: Difficulty concentrating, disbelief, preoccupation with the deceased, seeing/hearing them briefly.
Behavioral: Social withdrawal, crying, searching behaviors, avoiding reminders or seeking them.

All of these are normal. Grief has no timeline. First-year anniversaries, holidays, and milestones are often particularly hard.

## Supporting someone who is grieving

**What helps:**

- Show up. Physical presence. Just being there.
- "I'm so sorry for your loss." Simple acknowledgment.
- Specific offers: "I'm bringing dinner Tuesday — what does your family like?" (Not "Let me know if you need anything")
- Remember later: Check in at one month, three months. Most support disappears after the funeral.
- Let them talk about the person who died. Most grievers want to tell stories, not avoid the topic.
- Follow their lead: Some want distraction; some want to talk.

**What hurts:**

- "Everything happens for a reason"
- "They're in a better place"
- "At least they lived a long life"
- "You should be over it by now"
- "I know how you feel"
- Disappearing after the funeral

**Rule:** Witness the pain. Don't try to fix it.

## Complicated grief (when to seek help)

Consider professional support if, after 6-12 months, grief is:

- Dominating life without integration
- Causing significant functional impairment
- Involving thoughts of suicide or self-harm (seek help immediately)
- Complicated by traumatic circumstances of the death

Grief therapy, complicated grief treatment (CGT), and support groups are all effective options.

**Suicide loss:** A specific form that often involves shock, stigma, guilt, and searching for "why." Specialized support (survivors of suicide loss groups) is particularly helpful.
