# Business Law — Formation and Governance

## Choosing your business structure

| Structure       | Liability           | Taxes                        | Ownership            | Best for                             |
| --------------- | ------------------- | ---------------------------- | -------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| **Sole Prop**   | Unlimited personal  | Personal (Schedule C)        | 1 person             | Testing an idea, freelance           |
| **Partnership** | Unlimited (general) | Pass-through                 | 2+ people            | Professional practices (with care)   |
| **LLC**         | Limited             | Pass-through (default)       | Flexible             | Most small businesses                |
| **S-Corp**      | Limited             | Pass-through                 | ≤100 US shareholders | Profitable small businesses          |
| **C-Corp**      | Limited             | Corporate (double tax)       | Unlimited            | VC-backed startups, public companies |
| **B-Corp**      | Limited             | Same as underlying structure | Flexible             | Social mission companies             |

## LLC (Limited Liability Company)

**Why most small businesses choose LLC:**

- Personal assets protected from business debts and lawsuits
- Pass-through taxation (profits taxed on personal return, not twice)
- Flexible management structure
- Minimal formalities compared to corporations
- Can choose to be taxed as S-Corp when profitable

**Formation:**

1. Choose a state (Delaware for sophistication and flexibility; your home state for simplicity)
2. Choose a name (must include "LLC" or "Limited Liability Company"; check availability)
3. File Articles of Organization with the state ($50–$500 filing fee)
4. Get an EIN (Employer Identification Number) from IRS — free at irs.gov
5. Open a separate business bank account — CRITICAL
6. Draft an Operating Agreement

**Operating Agreement — the most important document:**
Governs the internal workings of the LLC.
Even single-member LLCs should have one.

Must address:

- Ownership percentages (membership interests)
- Management: member-managed vs. manager-managed
- How decisions are made and what requires unanimous consent
- How profits and losses are distributed
- What happens when a member wants to leave
- What happens if a member dies or becomes incapacitated
- Admission of new members
- Dissolution process

**Piercing the veil:** Courts can hold members personally liable if:

- LLC and personal finances are commingled
- LLC is used to commit fraud
- LLC is undercapitalized from the start
- Required formalities are ignored

**Prevent piercing by:**

- Maintaining a separate bank account for the LLC
- Never using LLC funds for personal expenses
- Documenting major decisions in writing
- Maintaining adequate capitalization

## Corporation (C-Corp and S-Corp)

**When to choose a C-Corp:**

- Raising venture capital (VCs require C-Corp, typically Delaware)
- Planning for an IPO
- Stock option plans for employees (ISOs require C-Corp)
- International investors
- Multiple classes of stock needed

**Delaware C-Corp:** The gold standard for startups.

- Court of Chancery specializes in business disputes — predictable outcomes
- Investor-friendly terms well-tested in courts
- No state tax on income earned outside Delaware
- Can be incorporated without a physical office in Delaware

**Formation:**

1. File Certificate of Incorporation with Delaware Secretary of State (~$89)
2. Appoint initial directors
3. Hold organizational meeting
4. Issue stock
5. Get EIN
6. File S-Corp election with IRS if applicable (Form 2553, within 75 days of formation)

**Corporate formalities (must maintain to preserve limited liability):**

- Annual shareholder meetings (or written consent)
- Annual director meetings (or written consent)
- Maintain minutes of all meetings
- Annual reports filed with state
- Separate bank accounts
- Corporate seal (optional but traditional)

**S-Corp:**

- Taxed like a partnership (pass-through)
- Avoid self-employment tax on "reasonable salary" portion
- Maximum 100 shareholders
- Only US citizens/residents can hold shares
- One class of stock only

**S-Corp tax advantage example:**
If business earns $200,000 profit and you pay yourself $80,000 salary:

- Self-employment tax applies only to the $80,000 salary
- Remaining $120,000 distributed as dividends (no SE tax)
- Saves ~15% SE tax on $120,000 = ~$18,000/year

## Equity and Ownership

### Cap table (capitalization table)

Tracks all equity ownership: who owns what percentage.
Keep this current from day one. Complexity only grows.

Simple example:

```
Founder 1:    500,000 shares   50%
Founder 2:    400,000 shares   40%
Employee pool: 100,000 shares  10%
Total:       1,000,000 shares  100%
```

### Founder vesting

Critical for co-founder teams: equity should vest over time.
Standard: 4-year vesting with 1-year cliff.

Cliff: No shares vest for the first year. All first-year shares vest at month 12.
After cliff: remaining shares vest monthly over 36 months.

Why: If a co-founder leaves at month 8, they take zero equity.
Without vesting, they'd walk away with whatever percentage was agreed upfront.

### Stock options (for employees)

Options give the right to buy stock at a fixed price (strike price) in the future.

**ISO (Incentive Stock Options):** Tax-advantaged for employees.
Requires C-Corp. No tax on grant or exercise (if holding period met).

**NSO (Non-Qualified Stock Options):** Tax on exercise (spread between strike and FMV).
Available to contractors, advisors, non-US employees.

**409A valuation:** Required before issuing options.
Sets the fair market value for strike price purposes.
Requires independent appraisal. Updated annually or after significant events.

### SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity)

Common early-stage investment instrument.
Investor gives money now; converts to equity at the next priced round.
Better than convertible notes for founders (no debt, no interest, no maturity date).

## Compliance Obligations

### Federal

- **EIN:** Required to hire employees, open bank accounts, file taxes
- **Federal taxes:** Quarterly estimated taxes (Form 941 if employees)
- **Annual tax return:** Form 1120 (C-Corp), Form 1065 (Partnership), Schedule K-1

### State and local

- **Annual reports:** Most states require annual LLC/Corp filings ($25–$300)
- **State taxes:** Income tax, franchise tax (California $800 minimum)
- **Sales tax:** Required if selling taxable goods/services in a state
- **Business license:** City/county license often required
- **Professional licenses:** Required for law, medicine, accounting, etc.

### Employment compliance (if you hire)

- Workers' comp insurance (required in almost all states)
- Unemployment insurance registration
- Payroll tax withholding and remittance
- Form I-9 for every new hire (employment authorization verification)
- New hire reporting to state

## Dissolving a Business

Properly dissolve to avoid ongoing fees and liability:

1. Vote to dissolve (per operating agreement/bylaws)
2. File Articles of Dissolution with the state
3. Cancel business licenses and permits
4. Close business bank accounts
5. Pay or settle all debts
6. Distribute remaining assets to members/shareholders
7. File final tax returns (mark "final return")
8. Notify IRS (close EIN if appropriate — usually don't close, just notify)

Failing to formally dissolve: some states continue charging annual fees
and franchise taxes indefinitely.
