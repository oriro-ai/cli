# Employment Law

## Employee vs. Independent Contractor — the most important distinction

Misclassifying employees as contractors is one of the most expensive
employment law mistakes. The IRS and Department of Labor use different tests,
but the core question is: **who controls the work?**

**Indicators of employee status:**

- You control when, where, and how work is done
- Worker uses your equipment/tools
- Work is integral to your core business
- Long-term relationship
- You set the hours
- Worker cannot work for competitors

**Indicators of contractor status:**

- Worker controls their own methods
- Worker provides their own tools
- Work is a distinct project, not ongoing operations
- Worker has multiple clients
- Worker can subcontract the work
- Worker invoices you

**The ABC Test** (used by California, New Jersey, and others — stricter):
A worker is an employee unless ALL THREE are true:
A — Free from direction and control in how they work
B — Work is outside the usual course of the company's business
C — Worker is customarily engaged in an independently established trade

**Penalties for misclassification:**
Back taxes, back benefits, penalties, overtime owed, workers' comp exposure.
California penalties can exceed $25,000 per violation.

## Hiring

### Job postings

- Do not specify age, sex, race, religion, national origin, disability
- Avoid: "recent graduate" (age discrimination), "native English speaker" (national origin)
- Focus on skills and requirements, not personal characteristics

### Interview — what you cannot ask

These questions can create discrimination liability:

| Forbidden topic    | Forbidden questions                                             |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Age                | "How old are you?" "What year did you graduate?"                |
| Religion           | "Do you observe any religious holidays?"                        |
| National origin    | "Where were you born?" "What's your accent?"                    |
| Marital/family     | "Do you have children?" "Are you married?"                      |
| Disability         | "Have you had any health problems?"                             |
| Pregnancy          | "Do you plan to have children?"                                 |
| Arrest record      | "Have you ever been arrested?" (convictions may be permissible) |
| Military discharge | "What type of discharge did you receive?"                       |

**What you CAN ask:**

- "Are you authorized to work in the US?" (not citizenship)
- "Can you perform the essential functions of this job?"
- "Can you meet the attendance requirements of this position?"

### Offer letters

Must clearly state:

- Position title and description
- Start date
- Compensation (salary or rate + bonus structure)
- At-will language (if applicable)
- Benefits summary
- Contingencies (background check, drug test)

**At-will employment:** Either party can end the relationship at any time,
for any reason, with no notice — unless a contract says otherwise.
Most US states are at-will. Exceptions: Montana (for-cause after probation),
and protections against termination for protected characteristics/activities.

## Wages and Hours

### Federal minimums (FLSA — Fair Labor Standards Act)

- Federal minimum wage: $7.25/hour (many states higher)
- **Always check your state/city minimum wage — often higher**
- Overtime: 1.5× rate for hours over 40/week (non-exempt employees)
- Pay period: must be regular and consistent

### Exempt vs. Non-exempt

**Exempt employees** are not entitled to overtime.
To be exempt, employees must meet BOTH:

1. Salary basis test: paid fixed salary ≥ $684/week ($35,568/year)
2. Duties test: executive, administrative, professional, outside sales, or computer

**Non-exempt employees** get overtime for every hour over 40/week.
When in doubt, treat as non-exempt — safer legally.

### Pay deductions — what's allowed

**Allowed:** Taxes, benefits the employee elected, court-ordered garnishments
**Not allowed:** Deductions that bring pay below minimum wage,
deductions for cash register shortages (in most states),
deductions for breakage or damage (in most states)

### Tip credits

Employers can pay tipped employees as little as $2.13/hour (federal)
IF tips bring total to minimum wage. Many states prohibit tip credits.
Tip pooling rules vary by state.

## Discrimination and Harassment

### Protected classes (federal law)

Title VII, ADA, ADEA, and other laws prohibit discrimination based on:

- Race, color, national origin (Title VII)
- Sex, gender identity, sexual orientation (Title VII per Bostock v. Clayton County)
- Religion (Title VII)
- Age (40+) (ADEA)
- Disability (ADA)
- Pregnancy (PDA)
- Genetic information (GINA)

Many states add: marital status, political affiliation, source of income, etc.

### Types of discrimination

**Disparate treatment:** Intentional different treatment based on protected class.
**Disparate impact:** Neutral policy that disproportionately affects protected class.

### Hostile work environment

Harassment is illegal when:

- Based on a protected characteristic
- Severe OR pervasive (one incident can be enough if severe enough)
- Creates an abusive work environment
- Employer knew or should have known and failed to act

Employer liability: If harasser is a supervisor → automatic liability.
If harasser is a coworker → liable if employer knew and failed to act.

### What employers must do

- Have a written anti-harassment policy
- Provide a reporting mechanism
- Investigate all complaints promptly and thoroughly
- Take prompt remedial action
- No retaliation against reporters

## Leave Laws

### FMLA (Family and Medical Leave Act)

- Applies to employers with 50+ employees
- Employees eligible after 12 months and 1,250 hours
- Up to 12 weeks unpaid, job-protected leave per year for:
  - Birth/adoption of child
  - Serious health condition of employee or immediate family
  - Qualifying military exigency
- Up to 26 weeks for care of injured servicemember
- Employee must be restored to same or equivalent position

### ADA and Reasonable Accommodation

Employers with 15+ employees must provide reasonable accommodation
for qualified employees with disabilities unless it causes undue hardship.

Accommodation examples: modified schedule, remote work, accessible equipment,
reassignment to vacant position, modified policies.

Interactive process: When employee requests accommodation, engage in dialogue.
Document the process. Never make unilateral decisions.

### State leave laws

Many states have paid family leave, paid sick leave requirements.
California, New York, New Jersey, Washington have robust paid leave programs.
Always check your state law — federal is the floor, not the ceiling.

## Termination

### At-will termination

You can terminate without cause and without notice (unless contract requires it).
But you cannot terminate for:

- Discriminatory reasons (protected class)
- Retaliation (for whistleblowing, reporting harassment, filing a complaint)
- Exercising a legal right (FMLA leave, workers' comp claim, jury duty)

### Final paycheck

Most states require final paycheck within days of termination.

- California: immediately on termination
- New York: next regular pay date
- Texas: within 6 days
  Accrued unused PTO may be owed depending on state and company policy.

### Severance

Not legally required (absent a contract or policy) but often paid.
If you offer severance, condition it on a signed release of claims.
Release must: be in writing, be in plain language, reference ADEA claims,
give 21 days to consider (45 days if group layoff), allow 7-day revocation.

### Documentation before termination

Before terminating for performance:

- Document the performance issues in writing
- Provide written warnings with opportunity to improve
- Apply policies consistently across all employees
- Record the termination decision and rationale

Inconsistent documentation or sudden termination after protected activity
is the most common path to wrongful termination claims.

### WARN Act (Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification)

Employers with 100+ employees must give 60 days notice before:

- Plant closing affecting 50+ employees
- Mass layoff of 500+ employees (or 50+ if 33%+ of workforce)
  Penalty: pay and benefits for the notice period.

## Non-Compete Agreements

### Enforceability by state

- **California:** Non-competes are void and unenforceable. Do not sign them; do not enforce them.
- **Florida:** Strongly enforced if reasonable in scope, duration, geography
- **Most states:** Enforce if: (1) necessary to protect legitimate business interest, (2) reasonable duration (usually 1-2 years max), (3) reasonable geographic scope
- **Federal:** FTC rule proposed banning most non-competes — check current status

### Trade secret protection is broader

Even without a non-compete, employees cannot take:

- Customer lists (if confidential)
- Proprietary processes
- Confidential business information
  This applies everywhere, regardless of non-compete enforceability.

## Practical rules

1. **Document everything.** Written warnings, performance reviews, disciplinary actions.
2. **Apply policies consistently.** The worst evidence in discrimination cases is inconsistency.
3. **Respond to complaints promptly.** Delay creates liability even if you take action later.
4. **Never retaliate.** Retaliation is often more expensive than the original complaint.
5. **Check your state law.** Federal is the floor; states often have stronger protections.
