# Chronic Disease Management

## Diabetes

### Type 1 vs. Type 2

**Type 1:** Autoimmune destruction of beta cells. No insulin production. Requires insulin for survival.
**Type 2:** Insulin resistance + declining beta cell function. Progressive. Most common form (90-95%).
**Prediabetes:** Fasting glucose 100-125 mg/dL or A1C 5.7-6.4%. Reversible with lifestyle.

### Blood sugar targets (ADA guidelines)

- Fasting/pre-meal: 80-130 mg/dL
- 2 hours after meals: < 180 mg/dL
- A1C: < 7% for most adults (less stringent for elderly/complex)

### Type 2 management hierarchy

1. **Lifestyle first:** Weight loss (even 5-10% significantly improves glucose), exercise, diet.
2. **Metformin:** First-line medication. Cheap, effective, well-tolerated. Take with food.
3. **Add-on medications:** GLP-1 agonists (semaglutide, liraglutide), SGLT-2 inhibitors (empagliflozin), DPP-4 inhibitors, sulfonylureas, insulin.
4. **Insulin:** Required if A1C not controlled with oral medications.

### Monitoring

A1C: every 3 months until target, then every 6 months.
Annual: eye exam (retinopathy), kidney function (creatinine, urine albumin), foot exam, lipids.
Blood pressure target: < 130/80 mmHg.

### Hypoglycemia (low blood sugar, < 70 mg/dL)

Symptoms: shaking, sweating, confusion, fast heartbeat, hunger.
Treatment (Rule of 15): 15g fast-acting carbs (glucose tablets, 4oz juice, regular soda).
Wait 15 minutes. Check blood sugar. Repeat if still < 70.
Severe (unconscious): glucagon injection or call 911.

## Hypertension (High Blood Pressure)

### Staging (ACC/AHA 2017 guidelines)

- Normal: < 120/80
- Elevated: 120-129/< 80
- Stage 1: 130-139/80-89
- Stage 2: ≥ 140/≥ 90
- Crisis: > 180/> 120

### Lifestyle modifications (proven to lower BP)

- **Weight loss:** ~1 mmHg per kg lost
- **DASH diet:** 8-14 mmHg reduction
- **Sodium reduction:** < 2,300 mg/day; < 1,500 for greater effect. 4-9 mmHg reduction.
- **Exercise:** 150 min/week moderate aerobic. 4-9 mmHg reduction.
- **Limit alcohol:** ≤ 1 drink/day (women), ≤ 2/day (men). 2-4 mmHg reduction.
- **Quit smoking:** Not directly antihypertensive but critical for cardiovascular risk.

### Medications

**ACE inhibitors** (e.g., lisinopril, enalapril): First-line for many. Side effect: dry cough.
**ARBs** (e.g., losartan, valsartan): Same mechanism, no cough. Used if ACE causes cough.
**Calcium channel blockers** (amlodipine, diltiazem): Effective, well-tolerated.
**Thiazide diuretics** (chlorthalidone, hydrochlorothiazide): Often first-line; older, inexpensive.
**Beta-blockers** (metoprolol, carvedilol): Especially for heart failure or post-MI.

**Note:** Never stop blood pressure medications without consulting your doctor.
Even if you feel fine — hypertension is usually asymptomatic until serious events occur.

## Heart Disease (Coronary Artery Disease)

### Understanding your risk

**ASCVD Risk Calculator** (ACC/AHA): Predicts 10-year cardiovascular event risk.
Factors: age, sex, race, total cholesterol, HDL, systolic BP, BP treatment, diabetes, smoking.

### Statin therapy

Statins reduce LDL and cardiovascular events.
High-intensity (atorvastatin 40-80mg, rosuvastatin 20-40mg): Reduces LDL by ≥50%.
Who benefits most: established heart disease, LDL ≥ 190, diabetes with risk factors, 10-year ASCVD risk ≥ 7.5%.

**Muscle symptoms (myopathy/rhabdomyolysis):** Uncommon but report to doctor immediately.
**Liver effects:** Routine monitoring not required; report jaundice or dark urine.

### Post-heart attack care

Medications for life after MI:

- Aspirin (81mg daily) unless contraindicated
- Statin (high-intensity)
- Beta-blocker (at least 1 year)
- ACE inhibitor or ARB (if reduced ejection fraction)
- P2Y12 inhibitor (clopidogrel, ticagrelor) for 1 year after stent

Cardiac rehabilitation: structured exercise program after MI. Significantly reduces mortality. Underutilized.

## Asthma

### Monitoring and severity

**Peak flow meter:** Track every morning. Personal best × 80% = green zone. Below 50% = red zone/emergency.
**Spirometry:** FEV1 and FVC ratio measures airflow obstruction.

### Medications

**Controller (daily, prevent symptoms):**

- Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS): fluticasone, budesonide — most effective controller. Use daily regardless of symptoms.
- Long-acting beta-agonist (LABA): salmeterol, formoterol. Never use alone; always with ICS.
- Leukotriene modifiers: montelukast (Singulair). Less effective than ICS but oral, useful add-on.
- Biologics: dupilumab, omalizumab for severe eosinophilic/allergic asthma.

**Rescue (quick relief):**

- Short-acting beta-agonist (SABA): albuterol — use for acute symptoms.
- If using rescue > 2 days/week: asthma is not well-controlled; step up controller therapy.

### Asthma action plan

Written plan with your doctor: what to do in green/yellow/red zones.
Green: peak flow > 80% personal best. Take controller as prescribed.
Yellow: peak flow 50-80%. Add extra rescue. Contact doctor if not improving.
Red: peak flow < 50%. Take rescue. Seek emergency care if not improving immediately.

## COPD (Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease)

Main cause: smoking (80-85%). Also: occupational dust, fumes, indoor air pollution.
Progressive and irreversible — but progression can be slowed.

**GOLD staging (based on FEV1):**
Grade 1 (mild): FEV1 ≥ 80% predicted
Grade 2 (moderate): 50-79%
Grade 3 (severe): 30-49%
Grade 4 (very severe): < 30%

### Management

**Most important:** Smoking cessation. The only intervention proven to slow progression.
**Vaccinations:** Annual flu, pneumococcal, COVID. Respiratory infections are major triggers.
**Pulmonary rehabilitation:** Structured exercise + education. Improves exercise tolerance and quality of life.

**Bronchodilators:**
LAMA (tiotropium, umeclidinium): Long-acting muscarinic antagonist. First-line for most.
LABA: Long-acting beta-agonist. Often combined with LAMA.
SABA/SAMA: Short-acting rescue inhalers.
ICS: For frequent exacerbations + eosinophilic inflammation.

**Oxygen therapy:** If O2 saturation consistently ≤ 88%. Use ≥ 15 hours/day to benefit.

## Arthritis

### Osteoarthritis (OA)

Degenerative joint disease. Cartilage breakdown. Wear and tear.
Most common in: knees, hips, hands, spine.
No disease-modifying treatment — manage symptoms.

**Management:**

1. Exercise: water aerobics, swimming, cycling. Strengthening around joint.
2. Weight loss: 10lb loss reduces knee force by 40lb per step.
3. Physical therapy: movement and strengthening strategies.
4. Acetaminophen (Tylenol): First-line analgesic. Max 3g/day (2g if liver disease).
5. NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen): Effective but GI/cardiovascular/kidney risks.
6. Topical NSAIDs (diclofenac gel): Less systemic absorption; good for knee OA.
7. Intraarticular corticosteroids: Temporary relief for acute flares.
8. Joint replacement: When conservative management fails.

### Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA)

Autoimmune inflammatory joint disease. Symmetrical small joints. Morning stiffness.
Aggressive early treatment prevents joint damage. Treat-to-target approach.

**DMARDs (Disease-Modifying Antirheumatic Drugs):**
Methotrexate: First-line. Weekly oral dosing. Requires folic acid supplementation.
Biologics (TNF inhibitors: etanercept, adalimumab; JAK inhibitors: tofacitinib): For inadequate methotrexate response.
