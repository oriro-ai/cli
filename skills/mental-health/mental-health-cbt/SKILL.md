# Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) Principles

## The CBT model

**Core principle:** It is not events that cause emotional distress — it is our interpretation of events.

```
Event → Automatic Thought → Emotion → Behavior → Consequence
         (often distorted)
```

**Example:**
Event: Friend doesn't text back for 2 days.
Automatic thought: "They must be angry with me. I did something wrong."
Emotion: Anxiety, guilt.
Behavior: Over-apologize, isolate, check phone constantly.

**Alternative interpretation:**
Event: Same — friend doesn't text back.
Balanced thought: "They're probably busy. I'll reach out once and give them space."
Emotion: Mild concern but not distress.
Behavior: Send one message, continue with your day.

**CBT teaches you to notice automatic thoughts, test them against evidence, and replace them with more balanced interpretations.**

## Cognitive distortions — the 15 most common

These are thinking patterns that systematically distort reality and generate unnecessary distress.

### 1. All-or-nothing thinking (black-and-white)

Seeing things as completely good or bad, with no middle ground.
"If I'm not perfect, I'm a total failure."
"This relationship is either perfect or it's worthless."

### 2. Catastrophizing (magnifying)

Expecting or assuming the worst-case scenario will happen.
"I made a mistake at work. I'm going to get fired. Then I won't be able to pay rent."
Fix: What is the actual probability? What is the most likely outcome?

### 3. Mind reading

Assuming you know what others are thinking (usually negative).
"She didn't smile at me — she must hate me."
"He thinks I'm incompetent."

### 4. Fortune telling

Predicting negative outcomes with certainty.
"This presentation will be a disaster."
"I'll never find a relationship."

### 5. Overgeneralization

Concluding one instance represents a universal pattern.
"This always happens to me."
"I never do anything right."
Signals: "always," "never," "everyone," "nobody."

### 6. Mental filter (selective abstraction)

Focusing exclusively on the negatives while filtering out positives.
One critical comment ruins 10 compliments.

### 7. Discounting the positive

Dismissing positive experiences as not counting.
"They only said I did well because they felt sorry for me."
"That was easy — it doesn't mean I'm good at this."

### 8. Should statements

Rigid rules about how you and others "should" behave.
"I should always be productive."
"People should always be fair."
Creates guilt (self-directed) and resentment (other-directed).

### 9. Labeling

Assigning global negative labels based on specific events.
"I'm a loser" instead of "I made a mistake."
"She's a terrible person" instead of "she did a hurtful thing."

### 10. Personalization

Blaming yourself for things outside your control.
"My friend is in a bad mood — I must have done something wrong."
"My child is struggling in school — I'm a terrible parent."

### 11. Emotional reasoning

Assuming feelings reflect reality.
"I feel stupid, so I must be stupid."
"I feel hopeless, so the situation is hopeless."

### 12. Jumping to conclusions

Reaching negative conclusions without sufficient evidence.

### 13. Magnification / minimization

Magnifying negative events, minimizing positive ones.

### 14. Control fallacies

Either: "I am responsible for everything" (overcontrol) or "I have no control over anything" (external control fallacy).

### 15. Blaming

Holding others responsible for your pain; or conversely, blaming yourself for everything.

## Thought records — the core CBT tool

A thought record examines an upsetting thought systematically.

| Column                | What to write                                 |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| **Situation**         | What happened? Who, what, when, where?        |
| **Automatic thought** | What was going through your mind?             |
| **Emotions**          | What did you feel? Rate intensity 0-100%      |
| **Evidence FOR**      | What supports this thought being true?        |
| **Evidence AGAINST**  | What contradicts this thought?                |
| **Balanced thought**  | A more realistic alternative                  |
| **Outcome**           | How do you feel now? Rate same emotions again |

**Example thought record:**

- Situation: Sent important email, no reply after 3 days.
- Automatic thought: "They're ignoring me because they don't respect me."
- Emotions: Anxious (70%), angry (50%).
- Evidence for: They haven't replied. We've had tension in the past.
- Evidence against: They have 200+ emails/day. There's a holiday week. They've always responded eventually.
- Balanced thought: "They're probably overwhelmed with email. I'll follow up once next week."
- Outcome: Anxious (20%), angry (10%).

## Behavioral activation

Depression and anxiety both involve behavioral withdrawal that maintains the problem.

- Depression: withdrawal from activities that gave you pleasure → less pleasure → more depression.
- Anxiety: avoidance of feared situations → short-term relief → maintains the fear long-term.

**Behavioral activation for depression:**

1. List activities you used to enjoy or that give a sense of accomplishment.
2. Schedule them in small doses, even when motivation is zero.
3. Track mood before and after.
4. Insight: action produces motivation, not the reverse.

**Exposure for anxiety:**

1. Create a fear hierarchy: situations ranked from least to most anxiety-provoking.
2. Gradually expose yourself to each level, starting at the bottom.
3. Stay in the situation until anxiety naturally decreases (usually 20-40 minutes).
4. Do not escape — escape reinforces the fear.
5. Over time, anxiety at each level decreases through habituation.

## Core CBT techniques for specific problems

### Anxiety and worry

**Scheduled worry time:**
Set aside 20-30 minutes/day for worry — only during this time.
When you catch yourself worrying outside this time, postpone: "I'll worry about that at 4pm."
Reduces worry's intrusion into the rest of your day.

**Decatastrophizing questions:**

- What is the worst that could realistically happen?
- What is the best that could happen?
- What is the most realistic outcome?
- If the worst happened, could I cope?
- What would I tell a friend in this situation?

**Physical anxiety:** Controlled breathing (4-7-8: inhale 4 counts, hold 7, exhale 8).
Reduces physiological arousal within 2-3 minutes.

### Depression

**Activity scheduling:** Don't wait to feel motivated. Schedule activities, rate pleasure and mastery (0-10) after.

**Pleasurable activities list:** Build a personal list. Commit to one small activity daily.

**Challenging hopeless thoughts:** Is this thought a fact or an interpretation?
What evidence exists that things will not improve?
What is the track record of my predictions about the future?

### Low self-esteem

**Self-compassion practice:**
Treat yourself with the same kindness you would offer a good friend.
Phrase: "This is a moment of suffering. Suffering is part of being human. May I be kind to myself right now."

**Positive data log:**
Each day write down evidence that contradicts your negative core belief.
Example: Core belief = "I'm incompetent." Log: "Solved a difficult problem at work. Friend asked my advice. Fixed the sink myself."

## When CBT is and isn't enough

**CBT is well-established for:**

- Generalized anxiety disorder
- Social anxiety
- Panic disorder
- Depression (mild to moderate)
- OCD (with ERP — Exposure Response Prevention variant)
- PTSD (with Trauma-Focused CBT or EMDR)
- Phobias

**Seek professional evaluation when:**

- Symptoms are severe or worsening
- You have thoughts of self-harm or suicide
- Symptoms interfere significantly with daily functioning
- Symptoms persist despite self-help efforts
- There may be biological/medication component (severe depression, bipolar, psychosis)

**Crisis resources:**

- 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline: call or text 988 (US)
- Crisis Text Line: Text HOME to 741741
- International Association for Suicide Prevention: https://www.iasp.info/resources/Crisis_Centres/
