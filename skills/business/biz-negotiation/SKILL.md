# Negotiation

## The foundational principle: focus on interests, not positions

**Position:** What you say you want. ("I want $100,000 salary.")
**Interest:** Why you want it. ("I need to cover rent, student loans, and feel valued for my skills.")

Most negotiations fail when both sides argue positions.
Successful negotiations explore underlying interests — often multiple ways to satisfy both.

**Example:**
Two people fighting over an orange.
Position-based: Split it in half.
Interest-based: One wanted the peel for a cake. One wanted the juice.
Solution: One gets the peel. One gets the juice. Both get everything they wanted.

## BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement)

The most important concept in negotiation.
Your BATNA = what you'll do if this negotiation fails.

**Why it matters:**
Strong BATNA = negotiating power. You can walk away.
Weak BATNA = pressure to accept bad terms.

**Before any negotiation:**

1. Know your BATNA precisely.
2. Estimate the other party's BATNA.
3. Never accept terms worse than your BATNA.

**Improving your BATNA:**
Get competing offers before negotiating. Have options. Don't be desperate.

**ZOPA (Zone of Possible Agreement):**
Range between each party's reservation value (worst acceptable outcome).
If ZOPA exists → deal is possible. If not → walk away.

## Salary negotiation

**Research first:**

- Glassdoor, Levels.fyi (for tech), LinkedIn Salary, Bureau of Labor Statistics
- Talk to people in the same role at similar companies
- Know the range before entering any conversation

**The rules:**

1. **Never give a number first if you can avoid it.** "I'd like to understand the full opportunity before discussing compensation. What is the budgeted range for this role?"

2. **If forced to give a number:** Give a range where the bottom of your range is your target.

3. **Anchor high.** First offers influence final outcomes disproportionately. Research shows people who anchor aggressively get better outcomes.

4. **Always negotiate.** 85% of employers have room above their first offer. Less than 50% of candidates negotiate.

5. **Total compensation, not just salary.** Negotiate: base salary, signing bonus, equity, PTO, remote work, title, start date.

6. **Don't accept immediately.** "I'm excited about this opportunity. I'd like to take 24 hours to review the offer thoroughly."

7. **Written offer before resignation.** Never resign from current job on verbal offer.

**Script for counter-offer:**
"Thank you for the offer — I'm genuinely excited about this opportunity. Based on my research into the market rate for this role and my [X years of specific experience], I was expecting something closer to $[X]. Is there flexibility to get closer to that number?"

## Multi-issue negotiations

**Package trades:** When you have multiple issues, trade across dimensions.
You might accept less on salary in exchange for more equity or flexibility.
Never give a concession on one issue without getting something in return.

**Logrolling:** Exchange low-value (to you) concessions for high-value gains.
Identify what matters most to each party. Trade across different priorities.

## Negotiation tactics — and how to respond

**Anchoring:** Opening with an extreme number to shift the range.
Response: Don't anchor off their number. Bring your own research and make your own anchor.

**Good cop/bad cop:** One negotiator is reasonable; the other is unreasonable.
Response: Recognize the tactic. Ask to speak directly with the decision-maker.

**Take it or leave it (false ultimatum):**
Response: "I understand. Let me take some time to think about that." Then negotiate again next day.
Most "final offers" aren't.

**Time pressure:** "This offer expires tomorrow."
Response: Real deals rarely expire in 24 hours. If it does, it probably wasn't right for you.

**Nibbling:** After deal is made, asking for small additional concessions.
Response: "We already reached agreement on the full package. I'm not comfortable reopening it."

**Silence:** Long pauses after you make a statement.
Response: Don't fill the silence. The discomfort is a tactic. Let it sit.

## Principled negotiation (Fisher & Ury)

Four principles from "Getting to Yes":

1. **Separate the people from the problem.**
   Be hard on the problem, soft on the people.
   Negotiation is not about winning against the other person.

2. **Focus on interests, not positions.**
   Explore the "why" behind demands.

3. **Invent options for mutual gain.**
   Brainstorm creatively before deciding. More options = better outcomes.

4. **Insist on objective criteria.**
   Base agreements on external standards (market rates, expert opinions, precedent).
   "That's not what I'm asking for, but let's look at what comparable deals look like."

## Negotiations that fail — why

1. Both sides bargain on positions with no exploration of interests
2. One party has no BATNA → accepts terrible terms
3. Emotional escalation → damage to relationship → walkaway
4. Information imbalance → one party doesn't know the market
5. Assuming the pie is fixed (distributive thinking vs. integrative)
6. Agreeing under time pressure before exploring options
